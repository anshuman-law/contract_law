SYSTEM MODEL

A recovery function inside a finance department handling five transfers: a duplicate vendor payment, a tax paid under a provision later struck down, a payment made under a threat to detain goods, a transfer made on a mistaken belief about an invoice's authenticity, and a payment made with full knowledge under protest. Readable state per transfer: whether it was made by mistake or under coercion, when it was made, what the recipient has done with the money since, and a Defence panel showing which defences are live.

runs a recovery programme across four quarters with a litigation budget. The physics is that s 72 states a broad rule and the contest happens in the defences, so the player's real work is anticipatory. Limitation runs from a point that depends on when the mistake was discovered, so the tax claim's viability turns on when the invalidity became known, and investigating that costs a move. Recipients who have changed their position in reliance on the receipt present a harder claim, and the player must decide whether to pursue quickly (before positions change further) or thoroughly (with better evidence). Three decisions shape the programme. First, sequencing: the duplicate payment is easy and small, the tax claim is large and slow, and the coerced payment depends on establishing that the detention was unlawful, which is Module 30's work and which the player must fund. Second, characterisation: the payment made under protest with full knowledge is not recoverable as a mistake, so the player must run it as coercion or not at all, and the quality of the protest record determines whether that is possible, which retrospectively justifies the discipline taught in Module 30. Third, the counterparty relationship, since the vendor who received the duplicate payment is a continuing supplier and an aggressive claim costs commercial value, so the player must weigh recovery against relationship. Quarter three introduces a recipient who has distributed the money onward, raising the question of how far s 72 reaches, which the game presents as an open area with a range of outcomes rather than a settled rule. Failure mode is a finance function that discovers all five transfers in quarter one, litigates them in order of size, and finds the two small easy ones time-barred by the time it reaches them.

Why the system resolved this way

CORE RUN COMPLETE

The controlling legal fact was identified.

DOCTRINAL SPINE

ICA s 72: a person to whom money has been paid, or anything delivered, by mistake or under coercion, must repay or return it. In Sales Tax Officer, Banaras v Kanhaiya Lal Mukundlal Saraf, AIR 1959 SC 135, the Supreme Court held that the word mistake in s 72 is used without qualification and comprises a mistake of law as well as a mistake of fact, so tax paid under a levy later held invalid was recoverable, and that s 72 does not conflict with s 21, since s 21 concerns a contract caused by a mistake of law while s 72 concerns a payment. Later authority has developed defences including limitation, and the interaction with change of position and unjust enrichment principles has been considered in subsequent cases, so the defences should be presented as an evolving area.

STRATEGIC PAYOFF

The player learns that s 72 is one of the widest provisions in the Act and that its breadth is disciplined by defences rather than by its own terms, and learns the contrast that Kanhaiya Lal had to spell out, that a payment made under a mistake of law is recoverable although a contract caused by one is not voidable. This closes the course by showing that obligations can arise without any agreement at all, which is where the ICA's quasi-contractual chapter has been pointing since Module 27.

WIN AND LOSS LOGIC

Win by recovering above the target across the four quarters within budget and without losing the key supplier relationship. Lose instructively by characterising a knowing payment as a mistake, or by letting limitation run on the easy claims while pursuing the hard one.

REPLAY HOOK

Replay where the recipient is a public authority, which raises different considerations about restitution of taxes and refunds, and shows that the identity of the payee changes the practical shape of the claim.
