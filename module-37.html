SYSTEM MODEL

A transaction desk processing four deals, each containing a mistake held by one side only: a mistake about the identity of the contracting party, a mistake about the nature of a document signed, a mistake about Indian law, and a mistake about a foreign law governing part of the deal. Readable state per deal: the route currently available (s 21, s 22, identity exception, non est factum, s 72 restitution, or none), and the value at stake.

runs an identity fraud scenario in depth, because that is where the exception has real work to do. The player has sold high-value goods on credit to someone who represented themselves as an established firm. The goods have been resold to an innocent third party. The player's objective is recovery, and the legal question is whether the contract was void for mistake as to identity (in which case no title passed and the third party's position is exposed) or merely voidable for fraud (in which case the third party who bought in good faith before avoidance is protected). This is a genuinely difficult and genuinely valuable distinction, and the game builds the entire endgame on it. Investigation moves establish the facts that drive the characterisation: whether the player intended to deal with the named firm specifically or with the person in front of them, whether the identity was material to the decision to give credit, whether checks were made against the firm's actual details, and whether the dealing was face to face or at a distance. Each fact moves the characterisation, and the player can shape it partly by what they emphasise, which is the interpretive axis operating on facts rather than on words. Meanwhile a clock runs, because the third party is about to resell again, and Claude plays their counsel with a policy of stressing the face-to-face elements to force a fraud characterisation. Round three offers a settlement from the third party priced at the midpoint, and the player must judge their own characterisation's strength. A parallel thread runs the mistake-of-law deal, where the contract cannot be avoided under s 21 but a payment made under it may be recoverable under s 72, and the player must notice that the two provisions coexist. Failure mode is a player who pursues unilateral mistake on the ordinary deals and exhausts the budget before reaching the identity claim.

DOCTRINAL SPINE

ICA ss 21 and 22. A contract is not voidable because it was caused by a mistake as to any law in force in India, but a mistake as to a law not in force in India has the same effect as a mistake of fact. A contract is not voidable merely because it was caused by one of the parties to it being under a mistake as to a matter of fact. Narrow case-law exceptions concern mistakes as to the identity of the person contracted with and as to the nature of the document signed. Note the contrast with ICA s 72, where the Supreme Court in Sales Tax Officer, Banaras v Kanhaiya Lal Mukundlal Saraf, AIR 1959 SC 135, held that 'mistake' includes mistakes of law, so money paid under a mistake of law is recoverable although a contract caused by such a mistake is not voidable.

STRATEGIC PAYOFF

The player learns why Indian law refuses unilateral mistake as a general vitiating factor (it would destabilise every transaction) and where it makes narrow exceptions (where there was never a meeting of minds at all), and learns that s 72 runs on a different track from ss 21 and 22, which is a distinction the cases had to work out expressly.

WIN AND LOSS LOGIC

Win by recovering the goods or their value from the third party on a sustainable characterisation, plus the s 72 payment. Lose instructively by treating s 22 as a general escape, or by conceding a fraud characterisation and losing priority to the innocent purchaser.

REPLAY HOOK

Replay with the dealing conducted entirely by correspondence, which strengthens the identity argument sharply and shows how much the face-to-face fact was carrying.
