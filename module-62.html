SYSTEM MODEL

A three-year equipment supply contract with delay-sensitive downstream commitments. Readable state: the stipulated sum; the player's provable loss on any given breach (hidden until the litigation phase); a proof-difficulty rating per breach type; whether the sum is held (deposit or earnest money) or merely payable; whether the clause recites a pre-estimate methodology; whether the sum reads as an alternative to performance; and a bargaining budget, since every clause the player hardens costs a concession elsewhere.

has two phases. Phase one is drafting, with four decisions and no dominant move. The number itself: because s 74 makes the sum a ceiling, a high figure buys nothing on the upside and invites the court to read it down, while a modest figure permanently caps recovery even where real loss runs far beyond it. The structure: forfeiture of money already held versus a sum payable on breach, since money in hand reverses who must sue and who must prove, though Kailash Nath applies s 74 scrutiny to forfeiture too and adds an arbitrariness constraint against a public authority. The evidentiary architecture: the player may spend bargaining budget embedding a recital of how the figure was calculated, which is the only move that hedges against a judge on the Kailash Nath line, because it lets the clause carry part of its own proof. And whether to make the sum the exclusive remedy, which feels like risk control but, under SRA s 23, surrenders specific performance if the sum reads as being in lieu of performance, and this contract has one component the buyer cannot source elsewhere. Phase two runs the clause through three escalating breaches, before a judge drawn at random from the Saw Pipes line or the Kailash Nath line, whose identity the player does not learn until after the draft is locked. A one-day delay with no material effect, where invoking the clause is legally available and strategically ruinous because it hands the opponent the penalty argument. A serious delay whose loss is genuine but awkward to quantify, which is the Saw Pipes zone and where the recital either saves the player or does not exist. And a breach where the supplier's default coincided with a market movement that left the buyer better off, which is the Kailash Nath fact pattern, no loss, and the ceiling collapses to nothing. Claude plays opposing counsel, attacking whichever limb the drafter left weakest and characterising a high number as a penalty. The failure modes are instructive rather than punitive: a clause read down to nil for want of loss, a ceiling capping a claim below actual damage, and exclusivity drafting that quietly extinguishes specific performance over the one asset that mattered.

Why the system resolved this way

CORE RUN COMPLETE

The controlling legal fact was identified.

DOCTRINAL SPINE

ICA s 74, under which the party complaining of breach is entitled, whether or not actual damage or loss is proved to have been caused thereby, to receive reasonable compensation not exceeding the amount named or the penalty stipulated. Fateh Chand v Balkishan Dass, AIR 1963 SC 1405, held that s 74 displaces the common law distinction between liquidated damages and penalty, so both are treated identically and the court awards reasonable compensation. ONGC v Saw Pipes Ltd, (2003) 5 SCC 705, held that where the parties agreed a genuine pre-estimate there may be no need to prove loss unless the court concludes no legal injury was suffered. Kailash Nath Associates v Delhi Development Authority, (2015) 4 SCC 136, held that damage or loss caused is a sine qua non for the application of s 74 and that the named sum is a ceiling and not a floor. The relationship between the Saw Pipes and Kailash Nath lines remains contested in current commentary and must be taught as unsettled. SRA s 23 provides that a stipulated sum is not a bar to specific performance unless the sum was named in lieu of performance; in Man Kaur v Hartar Singh Sangha, (2010) 10 SCC 512, the Supreme Court held that a party in breach could not resist specific performance merely because the agreement provided only for damages.

STRATEGIC PAYOFF

The player stops seeing a damages clause as a number and starts seeing three instruments fused into one: a ceiling (defensive), a burden-shifter (offensive, and only if the money is held), and a remedy-waiver (interpretive, and usually unintended). The last of these, the SRA s 23 exposure, is invisible to anyone who has read s 74 in isolation.

WIN AND LOSS LOGIC

Win by carrying the highest aggregate recovery across all three breaches while retaining specific performance over the critical component, under a judge whose doctrinal line the player did not choose. Lose instructively by drafting a clause that wins spectacularly under one line and returns nothing under the other, which teaches that s 74 drafting is risk allocation under interpretive uncertainty rather than arithmetic.

REPLAY HOOK

Replay as the supplier, drafting to be bound by a clause you intend to breach, where the goal is a ceiling low enough to function as a priced exit while still reading as a bona fide pre-estimate. Or replay with a public authority as counterparty, adding the Article 14 constraint Kailash Nath applied to the DDA's attempted appropriation.
