runs a twelve-week decision under commercial pressure. The player needs the asset, the defaulting seller is stalling, substitution costs rise weekly as the market tightens, and litigation is slow. The physics is the interlock: the routes are not parallel options but a decision tree in which early moves prune later branches. Serving the s 20 notice starts a thirty-day clock that costs nothing to start and preserves optionality, which most players discover too late to use. Three decisions carry the game. First, whether to serve the notice immediately, which is cheap and preserves the substitution route without committing to it, against the risk that it signals to the seller that the buyer will not seek performance. Second, whether to maintain readiness and willingness, which s 16 requires for specific performance and which costs money (keeping funds available, continuing to perform the buyer's own obligations) even while the seller is in default, and which Module 54 has already trained the player to document. Third, the timing of substitution: substituting early caps the cost but forecloses specific performance permanently under ss 14 and 16, and the asset is bespoke, so the substitute is imperfect. Claude plays the seller with a policy of stalling just enough to run the clock while occasionally signalling willingness, precisely to keep the buyer from committing to a route. Week nine introduces a third party who offers to buy the asset from the seller, which threatens the specific performance route entirely and forces a decision. Failure mode is a buyer who waits for performance, maintains readiness impeccably, and finds at week twelve that substitution now costs three times what it did in week two.

DOCTRINAL SPINE

SRA ss 10, 14, 16, 20 to 23 with ICA s 73. Following the Specific Relief (Amendment) Act, 2018, in force from 1 October 2018, s 10 provides that specific performance of a contract shall be enforced subject to sub-section (2) of s 11, s 14 and s 16, which the Supreme Court in B. Santoshamma v D. Sarala, (2020) SCC OnLine SC 780, confirmed makes specific performance no longer discretionary. Section 14 lists contracts not specifically enforceable, including where the party has obtained substituted performance under s 20. Section 16 bars relief in favour of a person who has obtained substituted performance or who fails to prove readiness and willingness. Section 20 introduces substituted performance: after giving notice of not less than thirty days calling on the party in breach to perform, the aggrieved party may get the contract performed by a third party or its own agency and recover the costs from the party in breach, and having done so cannot then seek specific performance.

STRATEGIC PAYOFF

The player learns that the 2018 amendment made specific performance a right rather than a favour, and simultaneously made it forfeitable by the claimant's own election, which is a genuinely modern structure and quite different from the pre-2018 position most textbooks still describe at length.

WIN AND LOSS LOGIC

Win by obtaining the asset or its economic equivalent at the lowest net cost including delay. Lose instructively by procuring substitution and then discovering the seller would have performed, or by holding out for specific performance while the substitution market moves away.

REPLAY HOOK

Replay for a fungible asset, where substitution is cheap and specific performance is unavailable under s 14 anyway, which shows how much the bespoke character was driving the whole problem.
